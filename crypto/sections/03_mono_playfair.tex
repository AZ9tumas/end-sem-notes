\section{Monoalphabetic Cipher}\label{sec:monoalpha}

The Caesar (shift) cipher has two weaknesses that make it useless. Its key space has only 25 useful keys, so an attacker simply tries them all. Worse, every letter is shifted by the \emph{same} amount, so the shift found for a single letter decrypts the whole message. The monoalphabetic cipher removes both weaknesses at once: instead of shifting the alphabet, it replaces each letter by an \emph{arbitrary} other letter, chosen independently for each of the 26 letters. The only constraint is that the replacement must be one-to-one, so that the receiver can undo it.

\subsection{Permutations}
A \imp{permutation of a finite set $S$ is an ordered sequence of all the elements of $S$, with each element appearing exactly once.} The set $S = \{a, b, c\}$ has six permutations: \ttx{abc, acb, bac, bca, cab, cba}. In general a set of $n$ elements has $n! = n(n-1)\cdots 2 \cdot 1$ permutations, because the first position can be filled in $n$ ways, the second in $n - 1$ ways (one element is used up), and so on down to one way for the last position. For example $2! = 2$ and $4! = 24$.

Permutations matter here because the key of a monoalphabetic cipher \emph{is} a permutation of the alphabet: writing the ciphertext letters for $a, b, \dots, z$ in order gives a sequence in which each of the 26 letters appears exactly once. The number of possible keys is therefore the number of permutations of 26 letters, $26!$.

\subsection{The cipher}
In a \textbf{monoalphabetic substitution cipher} Alice and Bob agree on a table that pairs every plaintext letter with one ciphertext letter. The bottom row of the table, the \textbf{cipher line}, can be any permutation of the 26 letters. To encrypt, each plaintext letter is looked up in the top row and replaced by the letter below it; to decrypt, each ciphertext letter is found in the bottom row and replaced by the letter above it. The relationship between plaintext and ciphertext symbols is \textbf{one-to-one}: a plaintext letter always becomes the same ciphertext letter, and no two plaintext letters share a ciphertext letter. If two letters shared one, the receiver could not tell which was meant, so the table would not be decryptable.

The key space has $26! \approx 4.03 \times 10^{26} \approx 2^{88}$ keys, compared with 25 for the shift cipher. A computer testing $10^{12}$ keys per second would need about 13 million years to try them all, so the slide's conclusion is that the large key space ``would seem to eliminate brute-force techniques''. The shift ciphers are just 26 of these $26!$ permutations, those in which the cipher line is the alphabet rotated. Although a permutation is involved, the monoalphabetic cipher is still a \textbf{substitution} cipher: the permutation is applied to the \emph{alphabet} to build the key, while the letters of the message keep their positions. Rearranging the message itself would be a transposition.

\subsection{Breaking it: frequency analysis}
The huge key space does not protect the cipher, because the attacker never searches through keys. A monoalphabetic cipher only \emph{renames} letters. If the key maps $x$ to $\pi(x)$, then every occurrence of $x$ in the plaintext becomes an occurrence of $\pi(x)$ in the ciphertext and nothing else becomes $\pi(x)$, so $\pi(x)$ occurs in the ciphertext exactly as often as $x$ occurs in the plaintext. The histogram of ciphertext letters is the histogram of plaintext letters with the bars relabelled. \imp{A monoalphabetic cipher does not change the frequency of characters}, so each letter of the key can be found separately by matching frequencies, and the attack needs a few dozen decisions instead of $26!$ guesses. This is a \textbf{statistical} (frequency) attack, and it needs only the ciphertext, which makes it a ciphertext-only attack, the most likely kind in practice.

The attack works because English letters are far from equally common. The relative frequencies (lecture chart, from a large corpus) are:

Pairs and triples are just as uneven. The most common \textbf{digrams} (two-letter groups) are \ttx{th, he, in, er, an, re, ed, on, es, st, en, at, to, nt, ha, nd, ou, ea, ng, as, or, ti, is, et, it, ar, te, se, hi, of}, and the most common \textbf{trigrams} are \ttx{the, ing, and, her, ere, ent, tha, nth, was, eth, for, dth} (lecture table, from Forouzan).

The attack therefore proceeds as follows. Eve counts the ciphertext letters and guesses that the most frequent one stands for \ttx{e} and the next for \ttx{t}. She then tests the guesses against digrams and trigrams: if \ttx{t} has been placed, a frequent pattern ``\ttx{t?e}'' is almost certainly \ttx{the}, which reveals \ttx{h}. Each new letter makes more words partly readable, and the remaining letters are filled in by recognising words. The statistics only become reliable with enough text: with about 100 letters the top two or three ranks usually match English, while the lower ranks are shuffled and have to be fixed by word guessing. For a very short message the counts may mislead completely.

\subsection{Pros, cons, countermeasure}
\textbf{Advantages.} The monoalphabetic cipher is as simple to use as the Caesar cipher, but its key space of $26!$ makes brute force impossible, and knowing one letter's substitute reveals nothing about the others. It is therefore much more secure than the Caesar cipher against an attacker who only tries keys.

\textbf{Disadvantages.} Since the ciphertext reflects the frequency data of the original alphabet, the cipher is easy to break with frequency analysis; the guessing attack above needs only a short ciphertext and some knowledge of English letter frequencies. The key is also long (a full table of 26 letters) and awkward to share and memorise, which is why practical versions derive it from a key phrase (see the homework below).

\textbf{Countermeasure: homophones.} A \textbf{homophonic substitution} gives each plaintext letter several possible ciphertext symbols, called \textbf{homophones}, and the encryptor picks one of them at random for each occurrence. The number of homophones is made proportional to the letter's frequency: \ttx{e} (about 13\%) gets about 13 symbols, while \ttx{z} gets one. Since each of the 13 symbols for \ttx{e} is then used for roughly $12.7/13 \approx 1\%$ of the text, every symbol appears with about the same frequency and the peaks in the histogram disappear. The ciphertext alphabet must be larger than 26, for example the numbers 00 to 99. Decryption is still unique, because each symbol belongs to only one plaintext letter.

Homophones flatten the \emph{single-letter} frequencies, but they do not make the cipher secure. Each plaintext letter still affects only one ciphertext symbol, so multi-letter patterns survive: \ttx{t} is still very often followed by \ttx{h}, so the symbols of \ttx{t} are often followed by symbols of \ttx{h}, and the attacker can group the symbols that behave alike and then proceed as before. Two better remedies were developed from this observation: encrypting \emph{several letters together} (Playfair, Hill), and using \emph{several cipher alphabets} chosen by a key (the polyalphabetic ciphers of \S\ref{sec:poly}).

\begin{mustknow}[: monoalphabetic cipher]
Key = any permutation of the 26 letters: $26! \approx 4 \times 10^{26}$ keys (Caesar: 25), so brute force is impossible. But the mapping is one-to-one and fixed, so \textbf{letter frequencies are preserved} and the cipher falls to frequency analysis: English ranking \ttx{e t a o i n s h r d l \dots}, top digram \ttx{th}, top trigram \ttx{the}. Homophones flatten single-letter frequencies but not multi-letter patterns.
\end{mustknow}

\subsection{Homework: encrypt with a key phrase}
Since a random 26-letter table is hard to remember, the key is often derived from a key phrase: write the phrase without spaces, delete every letter that has already appeared, and use the result as the cipher line. If the phrase does not contain all 26 letters, the unused letters are appended in alphabetical order (exactly as in the Playfair square below).

\section{Playfair Cipher \hothot}\label{sec:playfair}
\asked{2024--25 Q2 (3 marks): key ``GUIDANCE'', message ``The key is hidden under the door pad''. 2025--26 Q3 (4 marks): given key matrix, ``Insta Reels Pe Time Waste Na Kar'', filler X, I/J in one cell. Both solved in \S\ref{sec:pastpapers}.}
\likely{A third Playfair encryption or decryption (3--4 marks) with a doubled letter, an odd length and an I/J; or ``why is Playfair harder to break than a monoalphabetic cipher, and how is it still broken?'' (2--3 marks).}

The monoalphabetic cipher fails because every letter is encrypted on its own, so the letter frequencies of English pass straight into the ciphertext. The \textbf{Playfair cipher} attacks this at the root: it encrypts the plaintext \textbf{two letters at a time}, and the substitute of a letter depends on the letter it is paired with. It is the first literal \textbf{digram substitution} cipher, where a \textbf{digram} is a pair of consecutive letters treated as one unit. It was invented in 1854 by \textbf{Charles Wheatstone} but bears the name of \textbf{Lord Playfair}, who promoted its use, so it is also called the Playfair square or the Wheatstone--Playfair cipher. It is a manual \textbf{symmetric} technique that needs only pencil and paper, and it was used as a field cipher by the British army in World War I and by the Allied forces in World War II.

Caesar and the monoalphabetic cipher are \textbf{single-letter} ciphers: a plaintext letter always becomes the same ciphertext letter. Playfair is a \textbf{multiple-letter} cipher: the same plaintext letter becomes different ciphertext letters depending on its partner (in the solved question below, \ttx{e} becomes \ttx{D}, \ttx{O} or \ttx{N}), so single-letter frequencies are hidden. The slides (Forouzan) describe the same property in polyalphabetic terms: the key stream is a sequence of pairs $k = [(k_1, k_2), (k_3, k_4), \dots]$, each determined by a plaintext pair and the square, and $C_i = k_i$.

\subsection{Building the 5$\times$5 key matrix}
The secret key is an arrangement of 25 letters in a $5 \times 5$ matrix. Since the alphabet has 26 letters and the square has 25 cells, \textbf{I and J share one cell}. J is chosen because it is one of the rarest English letters (0.15\%) and a J written as I is easily corrected from context. A keyword makes the arrangement easy to remember and to share:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Write the keyword into the square row by row, \textbf{skipping every letter that has already appeared}.
  \item Fill the remaining cells with the unused letters \textbf{in alphabetical order}.
  \item Treat I and J as one letter throughout. If the keyword contains I or J, the I/J cell is filled at that point and any later I or J is a repeat.
\end{enumerate}

With the keyword \textbf{MONARCHY} the first eight cells hold M O N A R C H Y (highlighted), and the rest of the alphabet follows: B, D, E, F, G, I/J, K, L, P, Q, S, T, U, V, W, X, Z.

Any arrangement of the 25 letters is a valid key, so there are $25! \approx 1.55 \times 10^{25}$ possible squares and brute force is as hopeless as for the monoalphabetic cipher. (Rotating all rows, or all columns, cyclically gives the same cipher, because the rules only use ``same row'', ``same column'', ``to the right'' and ``below''; this divides the count by 25, which changes nothing in practice.)

\subsection{Step 1: split the plaintext into digrams}
Before encryption the plaintext is prepared: spaces are removed, every \ttx{j} is replaced by \ttx{i}, and the letters are split into pairs from left to right. Two adjustments are made while pairing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the two letters of a pair are the \textbf{same}, a filler (``bogus'') letter, normally \ttx{x}, is inserted between them, and the pairing continues from the second of the two letters.
  \item If, after all insertions, \textbf{one letter is left over} at the end, a filler \ttx{x} is appended.
\end{itemize}
The first rule exists because the encryption rules below are defined only for two \emph{different} cells. Two equal letters occupy the same cell, which lies in the same row and in the same column at once, so the row rule and the column rule would both apply and disagree, and the rectangle rule would have no rectangle. Splitting doubles also hides a give-away pattern: doubled letters (\ttx{ll, ee, ss, oo, tt}) are common in English, and if a double became a double in the ciphertext, an attacker would see where they are. The second rule exists simply because the cipher works on whole pairs. Only a double \emph{inside} one pair needs a filler; a double that falls across two pairs is harmless.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule

\midrule

balloon & \ttx{ba lx lo on} & \ttx{ll} in one pair: \ttx{l x}, then re-pair \ttx{l o o n}\\

\end{center}
In ``balloon'' the inserted \ttx{x} also moves the \ttx{oo} across a pair boundary, so it needs no filler of its own. The filler must be removed by the receiver after decryption, so it should be a letter that rarely occurs next to itself; if the doubled letter is \ttx{x} itself, another rare letter such as \ttx{q} or \ttx{z} is used.

\subsection{Step 2: encrypt each pair (three cases) \hot}
\likely{Every Playfair question: state the three rules and apply them; ``why is the rectangle rule its own inverse'' is a good 1--2 mark theory question.}
Each prepared pair is located in the square, and it falls into exactly one of three cases:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Same row:} each letter is replaced by the letter to its \textbf{right}, wrapping from the last column to the first.
  \item \textbf{Same column:} each letter is replaced by the letter \textbf{below} it, wrapping from the bottom row to the top.
  \item \textbf{Rectangle} (different row and different column): the two letters are opposite corners of a rectangle. Each letter is replaced by the letter \textbf{in its own row} and in the \textbf{other letter's column}, i.e.\ by the other corner on its own row. Order matters: \ttx{at}$\to$\ttx{RS} but \ttx{ta}$\to$\ttx{SR}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why each rule can be undone.} The receiver must be able to tell which rule was used and reverse it, so each rule must turn its own kind of pair into a pair of the \emph{same} kind, one-to-one.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Same row.} Moving one cell to the right with wrap-around is a cyclic rotation of the five letters of that row. A rotation is one-to-one and is undone by rotating to the left. Both ciphertext letters stay in the row and stay different, so the receiver sees a same-row pair and knows to move left. The wrap-around is essential: without it the last letter of a row would have no substitute.
  \item \textbf{Same column.} The same argument holds with ``down'' and ``up''.
  \item \textbf{Rectangle.} If the plaintext letters are at (row $r_1$, column $c_1$) and $(r_2, c_2)$ with $r_1 \ne r_2$ and $c_1 \ne c_2$, the ciphertext letters are at $(r_1, c_2)$ and $(r_2, c_1)$, the other two corners of the same rectangle. They are again in different rows and columns, so the receiver recognises the case, and applying the same rule to them gives $(r_1, c_1)$ and $(r_2, c_2)$, the original letters. The rectangle rule is \textbf{its own inverse}, which is why decryption uses it unchanged.
\end{itemize}
As a result, encryption is a one-to-one map on the $25 \times 24 = 600$ ordered pairs of distinct letters, which is exactly what decryption needs (checked by script for all four squares in this section). Three side effects follow from the rules, and cryptanalysts use them: a letter is never replaced by itself (every rule moves it to another cell); a reversed pair gives the reversed ciphertext (if \ttx{AB}$\to$\ttx{XY} then \ttx{BA}$\to$\ttx{YX}, as \ttx{at}/\ttx{ta} shows); and a ciphertext pair never consists of two equal letters.

\subsection{When I or J is in the keyword or the message \hot}
\likely{Both past questions contained an I (``hidden'', ``Insta''); the I/J cell and an \ttx{ii} double are where marks are lost.}
Since I and J share one cell, the cleanest procedure is to \textbf{replace every J by I before doing anything else}, in the keyword and in the message. Three consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the \textbf{keyword}, the first I or J takes the I/J cell; a later I or J is a \textbf{repeat} and is skipped, like any other repeated letter.
  \item In the \textbf{message}, a pair \ttx{ij} or \ttx{ji} becomes \ttx{ii}, which is a \textbf{double} and needs a filler.
  \item The ciphertext never contains J, because the cell is written as I. The receiver decides between i and j from context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Decryption}
Decryption prepares the ciphertext into pairs (no fillers are needed, since a ciphertext pair is never doubled and the length is even) and reverses each rule: a same-row pair moves one cell \textbf{left}, a same-column pair moves one cell \textbf{up} (both with wrap-around), and a rectangle pair uses the \textbf{same} rule as encryption, because that rule is its own inverse. Finally the receiver removes the fillers: an \ttx{x} between two equal letters or at the very end is almost certainly a filler, while a genuine \ttx{x} (as in ``taxi'') is kept. This last step needs judgement, which is a small weakness of the scheme.

\subsection{Strength and weakness}
\textbf{Strength.} Playfair is far stronger than a monoalphabetic cipher for three reasons. First, there are $26 \times 26 = 676$ possible digrams instead of 26 letters, so an attacker must identify individual digrams, which needs a 676-entry frequency table and much more ciphertext. Second, the frequencies of digrams are much flatter than those of letters: the most common digram, \ttx{th}, makes up only about 3--4\% of all digrams, compared with 12.7\% for the letter \ttx{e}, so the peaks that give a monoalphabetic cipher away are low and close together. Third, single-letter frequencies are hidden, because a letter's substitute depends on its partner. With $25!$ possible squares, brute force is also impossible. For these reasons Playfair was long considered unbreakable and served as a field cipher in both world wars.

\textbf{Weakness.} Playfair is still a \emph{monoalphabetic cipher on digrams}: a given plaintext pair always produces the same ciphertext pair (\ttx{th}$\to$\ttx{QK} twice in the solved question). The frequencies of digrams are therefore preserved, only slightly disturbed by the inserted fillers, and \imp{a cryptanalyst can use a ciphertext-only attack based on digram frequencies to find the key}. A few hundred letters of ciphertext are generally enough. The structure of the rules helps the attacker further: reversed pairs give reversed ciphertext pairs (a frequent pair such as \ttx{er}/\ttx{re} shows up as \ttx{XY}/\ttx{YX}), no letter maps to itself, and a known plaintext pair reveals which letters share a row, a column or a rectangle, from which the square can be rebuilt. Much of the structure of the plaintext language survives, which is why later ciphers moved to larger blocks (Hill) or to many alphabets (Vigen\`ere).

\begin{mustknow}[: Playfair]
\textbf{Key:} $5 \times 5$ square; keyword without repeats, then the rest of the alphabet in order; \textbf{I/J share one cell} (replace J by I first). Inventor \textbf{Wheatstone} (1854), promoter \textbf{Lord Playfair}; a \textbf{digram} (multiple-letter) cipher; $25!$ keys.\\
\textbf{Prepare:} split into pairs; a double inside a pair gets a filler \ttx{x} between the letters (re-pair from there); an odd last letter gets a filler \ttx{x}.\\
\textbf{Encrypt:} same row $\to$ right; same column $\to$ below (both wrap); rectangle $\to$ own row, other letter's column.\quad
\textbf{Decrypt:} left; up; rectangle unchanged (it is its own inverse); then drop the fillers.\\
\textbf{Security:} hides single-letter frequencies, but each digram always gives the same output, so digram frequencies leak.
\end{mustknow}
